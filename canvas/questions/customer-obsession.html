Customer Obsession — "Tell me about going above and beyond..."

Amazon's #1 Leadership Principle. Google calls it "Putting Users First." Every company values it. The trap: order-taking counts as "responsive," not "obsessed." The strongest answers are ones where you advocated for the customer against internal pressure — a PM wanted to skip a fix, or leadership wanted to ship faster, and you pushed back with user data.

Order-Taking vs Customer Obsession

Order-taking (weak)
Customer Obsession (strong)
  • "The customer asked for X, so I built X"
  • "I fixed the reported bug"
  • "I built the feature spec exactly as specified"
  • "I responded to support tickets quickly"
  • "I asked why they wanted X, discovered they actually needed Y, and built Y"
  • "I traced the bug to a class of issues and fixed the pattern"
  • "I pushed back on 2 requirements in the spec after talking to 5 users"
  • "I analyzed the ticket pattern and built self-service for the top-3 issues"

STAR Template

Situation

"Our [product/service/feature] was [customer-facing context]. I noticed that [customer pain point / user feedback / data showing friction]."

Task

"The expectation was [normal scope]. But I saw an opportunity to [exceed expectations / solve a deeper problem]."

Action

  1. Identified the customer need (not just the ticket/request)
    "I analyzed [support tickets / user sessions / feedback surveys] and found..."
    "I talked directly to [N] users to understand their workflow..."
  2. Proposed a customer-centric solution
    "Instead of just fixing the reported bug, I proposed redesigning [feature] because..."
  3. Advocated for the customer (even against internal pressure)
    "PM wanted to ship without the fix, but I presented data showing [impact on users]..."
  4. Delivered the solution
    "I implemented [what] and measured [customer metric]..."

Result

"Customer satisfaction increased from [X to Y]. Support tickets for this issue dropped by [Z%]. [N] users specifically mentioned this improvement in feedback."

Example: Push Back on PM

Example — Advocating for Users Against Pressure

Situation: We were shipping a new onboarding flow. Two weeks before launch, I noticed that the flow required users to enter their credit card in step 2 — before they'd seen the product.

Task: I wasn't the PM. My job was to build what was speced. But I'd talked to 8 users during discovery and knew this was going to tank conversion.

Action:
1. I pulled a week of session-replay data. 34% of new signups drop off at any CC-entry screen. Our current flow (CC at step 6) had that. The new flow moved it to step 2 — I estimated a 15-20 point drop in conversion.
2. I brought it to the PM in a 1:1, not in the group review. Framed it as: "I might be wrong about this — can we look at the data together?"
3. He initially disagreed — he had A/B test data from a different product showing early CC entry improved retention (of those who converted). Fair point.
4. I proposed a compromise: launch as a 20% experiment, with volume/conversion gates. If conversion dropped >10%, auto-rollback. If retention improved for converters, expand.
5. He agreed. I set up the gates and rollback triggers.

Result: Conversion dropped 18% in the first 3 days. Auto-rollback fired. We re-launched with CC at step 6 (original position). Retention among converters was actually unchanged. My data-driven pushback saved us from a launch that would have cost ~800K signups over the quarter. The PM later cited this as an example of "the right way to disagree."

"Customer" for Backend / Infra Engineers

If you're a backend or infrastructure engineer, you might feel like you don't have direct customer stories. You do — you just need to identify your customers.
  • Internal developers who use your APIs, services, or tools
  • End users affected by performance, reliability, availability
  • On-call engineers who debug with your logs and metrics
  • Other teams who depend on your system
  • Data scientists / analysts who query your data warehouse
Backend-engineer Customer Obsession example

"I noticed our internal API documentation was outdated and engineers were spending hours figuring out our endpoints. I talked to 4 consumer teams to understand where they got stuck. I built an auto-generated OpenAPI spec with runnable examples embedded in every endpoint doc. Integration time dropped from 2 days to 2 hours. Three teams sent unsolicited thank-you notes; one team said they'd been about to build their own workaround."

2

The "Direct Customer Contact" Signal

Amazon and Microsoft particularly reward stories where you talked directly to customers. Volunteer this early in your Action section:

  • "I did 5 user interviews..."
  • "I sat in on 3 support calls..."
  • "I shadowed the on-call engineer for a rotation..."
  • "I attended a customer advisory board..."
  • "I read the last 200 support tickets myself..."
Golden phrase: "I did X user interviews because I wanted to hear the problem in their words, not filtered through the PM."
3

Red Flags

"The customer asked for X, so I built X." — Order-taking, not obsession. Reframe: "The customer asked for X. I asked why, and discovered they actually needed Y."
No mention of customer impact in the Result. — Just technical metrics won't cut it. Add: NPS delta, ticket reduction, adoption rate, unsolicited feedback.
"I didn't interact with customers directly." — Find indirect customer impact. Even backend/infra work has downstream customers.
Building what you think is cool vs what customers need. — Interviewers spot this instantly. Anchor every decision to user evidence.
4

Follow-Up Questions to Prepare

"How do you know the customers actually wanted this?"

"I ran 5 user interviews and 3 mentioned this problem unprompted. I also had ticket data showing 40% of tickets in this feature were the same underlying issue. The signal was consistent across qual and quant."

"What if the customer had been wrong about what they needed?"

"That's why I probed with 'why' questions instead of just implementing the ask. In one interview, the user said 'I need faster search.' Asking why revealed the actual problem was that stale results were more frustrating than slow results — a different fix entirely."

"How did you convince the PM?"

"I brought the data privately in a 1:1 first, framed as 'let's look at this together' rather than 'I disagree.' When I proposed an experiment with a rollback trigger, it gave him a way to be wrong without losing face — and it made the decision reversible."

"What was the customer impact if you'd been wrong?"

"Worst case, we'd have delayed the feature by 2 weeks and gotten valuable A/B data. Best case, we avoided an 18-point conversion drop. The asymmetry favored testing. That framing is what unblocked the PM."

5

Amazon-Specific Framing

Amazon interviewers listen for these specific patterns:

  • "Working backwards from the customer" — start from customer outcome, work back to implementation
  • "PR/FAQ" mention — Amazon's product doc format that starts with the press release
  • "Customer anecdotes and metrics differ" — dive deep when data and stories disagree
  • "Earn and keep customer trust" — long-term over short-term revenue
Best story for Amazon: a time you optimized for long-term customer trust at the cost of a short-term revenue metric. That's the archetype Bezos wrote about in the '97 shareholder letter.
