Conflict Resolution — "Tell me about a disagreement..."

This is the #1 most-asked behavioral question across all FAANG companies. Every loop, in some form, includes it. Interviewers want to see: (1) you listen before you argue, (2) you disagree with data not emotion, (3) you commit fully after a decision is made, and (4) your relationship improved as a result.

STAR Template

Situation (2-3 sentences)

"On [project], I was working with [role] on [what]. We had a disagreement about [specific technical/product/process decision]."

Think: What was at stake? Why did it matter?

Task

"I needed to [resolve the disagreement / find common ground / make a decision] because [impact if unresolved — deadline, quality, customer impact]."

Action — this is 70% of your answer

  1. Listened first — "I set up a 1:1 to understand their perspective. They felt that..."
  2. Acknowledged their view — "I agreed that their concern about X was valid..."
  3. Shared your perspective with data — "I shared data showing that approach Y would..."
  4. Found common ground or escalated respectfully — "We agreed to prototype both approaches and let the data decide..."
  5. Committed to the outcome — "Once we decided on approach Z, I fully committed even though it wasn't my first choice..."

Result

"We shipped [outcome] on time. The approach we chose resulted in [metric]. More importantly, my relationship with [person] actually improved — we became go-to collaborators after that."

Learnings (for Google)

"I learned that starting with listening instead of arguing saves time. Now I always seek to understand before seeking to be understood."

2

Example: Full Answer

Example — Strong Conflict Story (~2 min spoken)

Situation: I was tech lead on a new payments service. Our staff engineer wanted to build it as a monolith. I believed we needed to split it into 3 services because the compliance requirements for card data were fundamentally different from the wallet balance flows.

Task: I had one week before the design review. If we went into the review misaligned, we'd delay the whole quarter's roadmap.

Action:
1. I asked for a 45-minute 1:1 with the staff engineer, framed as "I want to understand your reasoning" — not "let me convince you."
2. He said the monolith would ship 6 weeks faster and reduce operational surface area. Both were true.
3. I acknowledged both explicitly. Then I shared a one-pager showing that the PCI compliance scope for the monolith would include the wallet team's data, which would force them to re-certify — a 4-month process. He hadn't considered the org-wide compliance cost.
4. Rather than pushing my preferred split, I proposed we scope out both options and let the compliance team weigh in. He agreed.
5. Compliance confirmed the concern. We landed on a 2-service split (payments + wallet) rather than my original 3. It wasn't my first choice, but I committed fully — I wrote the migration plan for the merged wallet service myself over the weekend so the team could hit the deadline.

Result: We shipped the split in 10 weeks with zero compliance findings. The staff engineer and I have since collaborated on 4 more projects. He specifically requested me as tech lead for the next one.

Learnings: Framing the initial conversation as "help me understand" instead of "let me convince you" changed everything. When people don't feel attacked, they show you the parts of the problem they've been thinking about that you haven't.

Instant Red Flags

"I just avoided the person after that." — Shows no resolution skills. Instant downgrade.
"My manager had to step in." — Shows you can't resolve peer-to-peer. Only OK if there was a true escalation-worthy issue (ethics, safety).
"They were clearly wrong." — Shows no empathy or humility. Even if true, framing it this way is a fail.
"I escalated to their manager." — Shows poor peer relationships. Only acceptable in extreme cases and only after multiple direct attempts.
No quantified result. — Story feels like a vent, not a professional example. Add: "The relationship improved" or "We shipped X on time."

Follow-Up Questions to Prepare

"What if they still disagreed after your data?"

"If we still couldn't align after data, I'd propose we run a small, time-boxed experiment. If that also didn't resolve it, I'd write up both positions and escalate together — not going around them."

"What would you do differently?"

"I'd have proposed the 1:1 within 24 hours instead of waiting three days. The delay caused the disagreement to spread through Slack before I could contain it."

"How do you know they respected your pushback?"

"Two signals: (1) they specifically requested me for their next project, (2) they used our conversation as a case study in their own promotion doc — I asked."

"Have you ever escalated to a manager?"

"Once. A peer was making a decision that would violate our compliance boundary and refused to hear the concern. I looped in both our managers in a shared doc — not a Slack ping — with the specific policy citation. Escalation should be transparent, not sneaky."

The "Disagree and Commit" Framing

Amazon interviewers love hearing the phrase "disagree and commit" naturally in your answer. The two-part structure:

  1. Disagree: "I shared my concern with data and pushed back respectfully."
  2. Commit: "Once the decision was made, I committed fully — including [specific action showing you didn't sabotage]."
Best signal of commit: Volunteering to do the hardest part of the decision you disagreed with. "I wrote the migration plan myself" or "I owned the launch communications."
