Ambiguity & Decisions — "Tell me about incomplete information..."

This category separates Senior from Staff. Mid-level engineers follow direction. Senior engineers execute against a spec. Staff engineers create direction from ambiguity — they define the problem when nobody else can. Google specifically evaluates "Comfort with Ambiguity" as a core Googleyness trait.

STAR Template — Under Ambiguity

Situation

"We were starting [project/initiative] but [specific ambiguity — no spec, unclear requirements, conflicting stakeholder needs, new domain with no expertise]."

Task

"I needed to [define the approach / make a decision / unblock the team] despite [what was unclear]."

Action

  1. Identified what you DID know vs didn't know
    "I listed the known constraints and the open questions..."
  2. Gathered just enough information (not all)
    "I talked to [3 key people] to understand [specific things]..."
    "I researched [comparable systems/past incidents/industry patterns]..."
  3. Made a reversible decision (Bias for Action)
    "I proposed we start with [approach] because it was low-risk and reversible..."
    "I defined clear success criteria so we'd know within [timeframe] if this was working..."
  4. Set up checkpoints to validate
    "I scheduled a review after [2 weeks / first milestone] to course-correct..."
  5. Communicated tradeoffs transparently
    "I documented the decision, the risks, and the alternatives we considered..."

Result

"We shipped [what] in [timeframe]. The approach I chose turned out to [work well / need adjustment]. The checkpoint process meant we caught [issue] early and pivoted without wasting [time/effort]."

Example: Full Answer

Example — Ambiguity Story

Situation: Our team was asked to "add AI capabilities" to our developer tool. No PM, no spec, no design — just a directive from leadership.

Task: I needed to translate that into a shippable plan within 3 weeks so we could commit for the quarter.

Action:
1. I listed what we knew: our users were developers, our tool had 40K DAU, and leadership wanted "AI." I listed what we didn't know: which AI use case, what latency budget, which model, what data.
2. Rather than trying to answer all of those, I talked to 5 users in 2 days. Three of them independently mentioned "help me write better commit messages." That gave me a concrete use case.
3. I proposed we build a commit-message-suggestion feature as a two-week POC — reversible, low-risk, no infra commitment. I defined success: 30% adoption in the first month among the beta group.
4. I set a checkpoint at week 2 to decide: ship as GA, iterate, or kill. I made the kill option explicit so leadership wouldn't feel sunk-cost.
5. I documented the alternatives I considered (code review, chatbot, refactoring assist) and why I ruled them out. The doc got read by 3 other teams also being asked to "add AI."

Result: Shipped in 3 weeks. 42% adoption in month 1. More importantly, my scoping doc became the template 2 other teams used to define their AI initiatives — leadership specifically cited it in the quarterly review as a good pattern for ambiguous asks.

Decision Frameworks to Mention

One-Way vs Two-Way Doors (Amazon)

One-way door: Irreversible, high-stakes → take your time, gather data.

Two-way door: Reversible, can undo → decide quickly, iterate.

Golden phrase: "I recognized this was a two-way door, so I decided in 24 hours and set a 2-week checkpoint."

The 70% Rule (Jeff Bezos)

"If you wait for 90% of the information, you're too slow."

"Most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you wish you had."

If you make a bad two-way-door decision, cost is low. Analysis paralysis is the bigger risk.

RAPID Framework

  • Recommend — who proposes?
  • Agree — who has veto?
  • Perform — who executes?
  • Input — who provides info?
  • Decide — who makes the final call?

Useful when explaining how you unblocked a stuck cross-team decision.

Impact vs Effort Matrix

For prioritization questions:

  1. High impact, low effort → do first
  2. High impact, high effort → schedule carefully
  3. Low impact, low effort → maybe do
  4. Low impact, high effort → don't do
2

Prioritization Answer Pattern

When asked "How did you prioritize?", walk through your criteria:

  1. Customer impact — which option affects the most users?
  2. Reversibility — do the irreversible thing first (can iterate on the rest)
  3. Dependencies — what blocks other teams?
  4. Data — what do the metrics say?
  5. Impact vs Effort — ROI ranking
Naming a specific framework signals seniority. "I used a weighted scoring model on impact, effort, and reversibility" is more credible than "I just prioritized by importance."
3

Red Flags

"I asked my manager what to do." — At Senior+ this is a hard fail. You should be giving your manager recommendations, not asking for direction.
"I waited for the requirements to become clear." — Shows passivity. Instead: "I proposed a scoping session and drove it."
No mention of tradeoffs. — If you don't articulate what you gave up, interviewers assume you didn't consider alternatives.
Perfect outcome with no course-correction. — Ambiguity stories should include at least one moment where you re-evaluated. Perfect linear execution sounds fake.

Follow-Up Questions to Prepare

"What if you had been wrong?"

"I built in a 2-week checkpoint specifically for that. If adoption was below 15%, we'd have killed it and pivoted to option B (code review assist). I wanted to make the kill option cheap."

"How did you know 70% was enough info?"

"Because the decision was reversible (2-way door). Waiting another week to talk to 5 more users would have cost me 1/6th of the quarter. The delay cost was higher than the decision-quality upside."

"What did you learn about ambiguity?"

"The instinct to gather more data before deciding usually feels like diligence but is often procrastination. Now I explicitly ask 'is this a 1-way or 2-way door?' and set a hard time-box on 2-way-door decisions."

"How did you know when to pivot?"

"I defined the pivot criteria upfront — 30% adoption in month 1. That let me evaluate the decision on the data, not on how much time I'd invested. Sunk-cost thinking is the enemy of ambiguity."
