Teamwork & Collaboration — "Tell me about a cross-functional project..."

Cross-functional work reveals more than technical skill — it shows how you adapt your communication, build alignment across teams, and help others succeed. Google's #2 Googleyness trait is Collaborative Nature. Amazon wants Earn Trust + Hire & Develop. But watch out at Netflix — they explicitly warn against over-indexing on collaboration for its own sake.

Three Story Archetypes to Prepare

1. Cross-functional project

You aligned engineering, PM, design, data science. Adapted communication for each audience.

2. Helping a struggling teammate

You noticed someone stuck (or falling behind). You stepped in without being asked and enabled them.

3. Giving difficult feedback

You told a peer something they didn't want to hear. Used SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact). Relationship improved.

1

Template 1 — Cross-Functional Project

Situation

"I was working on [project] that required collaboration between [engineering, PM, design, data science, etc.]. The challenge was [competing priorities / different technical opinions / communication gaps]."

Task

"I needed to [align the teams / bridge the communication gap / deliver a cross-team deliverable]."

Action

  1. Understood each team's perspective — "I scheduled 1:1s with each stakeholder to understand their constraints and priorities..."
  2. Found common ground — "I identified we all agreed on [shared goal] even though we disagreed on [approach]..."
  3. Adapted communication — "I translated technical concepts for the PM using [analogies/diagrams] and business impact for engineering using [metrics]..."
  4. Created alignment mechanisms — "I set up a weekly sync with shared [doc/dashboard/Slack channel] to track progress..."
  5. Delivered together — "I coordinated the integration across teams, ensuring [no blocking dependencies]..."

Result

"We shipped [deliverable] on time. The cross-team collaboration model I set up was later adopted for [other projects]."

2

Template 2 — Helping a Struggling Teammate

  • S "I noticed [teammate] was struggling with [specific challenge — new codebase, complex feature, personal circumstances]..."
  • T "Nobody had explicitly asked me to help, but I decided to step in because [impact on team/project if they continued struggling]..."
  • A "I [paired with them / set up 1:1 sessions / broke the problem into smaller pieces / shared relevant resources]. Specifically, I..."
  • R "Within [timeframe], they were able to [deliver the feature / gain confidence / become independent]. They later [specific outcome]."
Key signal: Show it was YOUR initiative, not assigned by a manager. The strongest version: "I noticed X, my manager hadn't asked, and I decided to invest."
3

Template 3 — Giving Difficult Feedback (SBI model)

SBI = Situation, Behavior, Impact. A widely-used feedback framework. Mention it by name.

  • S "A peer's [code quality / approach / behavior] was impacting [team/project/quality]..."
  • A "I gave feedback using the SBI framework:
    • Situation: 'In yesterday's code review...'
    • Behavior: 'I noticed the error handling was missing for the API calls...'
    • Impact: 'This could cause silent failures in production...'
  • R "They appreciated the feedback and fixed the issue. Our code quality improved and they've since adopted the same approach when giving feedback to others."
Key signal: The feedback was delivered in private, was specific and observable (not "you're not detail-oriented enough"), and the relationship strengthened as a result.
4

Example: Adapting Communication Style

Example — Adapted Communication for 3 Audiences

Situation: I was leading a database migration that touched 3 teams: engineering (owned the code), the PM (owned the roadmap), and data science (relied on the analytics warehouse).

Task: Each team needed a different level of detail. The engineering team wanted the migration ADR. The PM wanted the impact on the roadmap. Data science wanted to know if their dashboards would break.

Action:
1. For engineering, I wrote a 6-page technical ADR with schema diagrams, migration steps, and rollback procedures. Reviewed live in a working session.
2. For the PM, I made a one-slide summary: 3 weeks of engineering, 0 external-facing feature impact, unlocks feature X in Q3. No ADR — she doesn't need it.
3. For data science, I made a checklist of every dashboard query that touched the affected tables, with the specific change each would need. I ran a 30-minute office-hour session to walk them through.
4. Each communication took me ~2 hours to prep, but saved days of confusion. When the actual migration ran, no team was surprised.

Result: Migration completed in 3 weeks with zero rollbacks or emergency pings. The PM cited the one-slide summary as the format she wanted from every tech lead going forward. Data science reported zero broken dashboards post-migration — usually they'd have 2-3.

1

Red Flags

"I did most of the work." — Sounds like a poor collaborator. Even if true, don't say it. Reframe: "I owned X while my teammates owned Y and Z."
"The other team was difficult." — Blame-shifting. Reframe: "The other team had different priorities — I bridged them by..."
"My manager handled the alignment." — Show YOU drove collaboration, not that you passed it up.
No mention of adapting communication style. — Signals one-size-fits-all communicator. Deliberately name at least one style shift.
"I helped everyone." — Vague. Pick one specific person or team, one specific action.
2

Netflix-Specific Caveat

Watch out at Netflix: Their culture memo explicitly warns against "team player for its own sake." They want high-performing individuals who also collaborate, not people who prioritize consensus over impact. For Netflix, don't over-index on "I made sure everyone agreed." Instead: "I made the call, communicated it clearly, and the team executed."

Follow-Up Questions to Prepare

"How did you handle the teammate who didn't want your help?"

"I didn't push. I made the offer clear ('happy to pair on this if useful') and let them come to me. Two weeks in they did. The trust they extended because I hadn't imposed made the pairing more effective."

"What did you learn from a difficult teammate?"

"I learned that 'difficult' usually means their incentives don't match mine. Once I asked what their team was being measured on this quarter, I understood why they were pushing back. Then I could reframe my ask around their metric."

"Have you ever failed at a cross-team collaboration?"

"Yes. Early on I sent the same 6-page ADR to a PM who needed a one-slide summary. She stopped opening my docs. It took a candid conversation where she told me directly. Now I always ask 'what format works for you?' upfront."

"How did giving that feedback affect the relationship?"

"They initially got defensive — expected. I asked them what THEY thought went wrong, which shifted us from 'me judging' to 'us diagnosing.' A month later they gave ME feedback that turned out to be right. That's when I knew trust had increased."

4

The "Onboarding" Variant

"Describe a time you onboarded onto a new team or codebase" is really testing your ability to bootstrap in ambiguity. Structure:

  1. Week 1 — read + observe: "I set myself a goal to attend every recurring meeting for the first two weeks without proposing anything, just listening."
  2. Ramp signal: "I asked to be assigned a small end-to-end task to make sure I'd touch every layer of the stack."
  3. Ask the "dumb" questions early: "I made a doc of every acronym and abbreviation and asked my onboarding buddy to fill it in — turns out 3 other new-hires used it."
  4. First deliverable: "By week 4 I shipped X (specific measurable). This is 2 weeks faster than the team's average ramp."
  5. Give back: "I updated the onboarding doc with everything I'd figured out. The next 3 hires used it and ramped 30% faster."
